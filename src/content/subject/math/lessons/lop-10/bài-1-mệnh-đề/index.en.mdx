## Propositions and Predicates

<Youtube url="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=example_menh_de"/>

### a) Propositions

**Definition:** Each logical proposition (referred to simply as a proposition) is an assertion that is either true or false.

**Truth value property:** Each proposition must be either true or false. A proposition cannot be both true and false.

**Notation:** Capital letters $P, Q, R,...$ are commonly used to denote propositions.

**Non-propositions:** Interrogative sentences (questions), exclamatory sentences, and imperative sentences (commands, requests) are not propositions.

**Mathematical propositions:** Propositions concerning mathematics are called mathematical propositions.

### b) Predicates (Propositional Functions)

**Definition:** A predicate (or propositional function) is an assertive sentence containing one or more variables whose truth value is not yet determined; upon replacing the variables with specific values from a given set, a proposition is obtained.

**Example:** Consider the sentence "$n$ is divisible by 2" (where $n \in \mathbb{N}$):

• For $n = 5$, we obtain the proposition "5 is divisible by 2" (this is a false proposition).

• For $n = 10$, we obtain the proposition "10 is divisible by 2" (this is a true proposition).

## Negation of a Proposition

**Definition:** To negate a proposition $P$, one typically adds (or removes) the word "not" before the predicate of proposition $P$. The negation of proposition $P$ is denoted by $\overline{P}$.

**Truth value rule:** Proposition $P$ and proposition $\overline{P}$ are two contradictory statements. If $P$ is true, then $\overline{P}$ is false; and if $P$ is false, then $\overline{P}$ is true.

**Example:** Proposition $P$: "17 is a perfect square". Its negation is $\overline{P}$: "17 is not a perfect square" ($\overline{P}$ is true).

## Conditional Propositions and Converse Propositions

### a) Conditional Propositions

**Definition:** Let $P$ and $Q$ be two propositions. The proposition "If $P$, then $Q$" is called a conditional proposition (or material implication) and is denoted by $P \Rightarrow Q$.

**Truth value condition:** The proposition $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. Therefore, we only need to evaluate the truth value of proposition $P \Rightarrow Q$ when $P$ is true. In that case, if $Q$ is true, then $P \Rightarrow Q$ is true; if $Q$ is false, then $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false.

**Mathematical theorems:** Mathematical theorems are true propositions and usually have the form $P \Rightarrow Q$. In this case:

• $P$ is the hypothesis (premise) of the theorem, and $Q$ is the conclusion of the theorem;

• $P$ is a sufficient condition for $Q$;

• $Q$ is a necessary condition for $P$.

### b) Converse Propositions

**Definition:** The proposition $Q \Rightarrow P$ is called the converse of the proposition $P \Rightarrow Q$.

**Remark:** The converse of a true proposition is not necessarily true.

**Example:** Proposition: "If triangle $ABC$ is equilateral, then triangle $ABC$ is isosceles" (true). Converse proposition: "If triangle $ABC$ is isosceles, then triangle $ABC$ is equilateral" (false).

## Biconditional Propositions

**Definition:** The proposition "$P$ if and only if $Q$" is called a biconditional proposition (or equivalence) and is denoted by $P \Leftrightarrow Q$.

**Truth value condition:** If both propositions $P \Rightarrow Q$ and $Q \Rightarrow P$ are true, then the biconditional proposition $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is true.

**Equivalent formulations:** When $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ is true, we have equivalent formulations:

• "$P$ is equivalent to $Q$";

• "$P$ is a necessary and sufficient condition for $Q$";

• "$P$ if and only if $Q$" (or "$P$ iff $Q$").

## Propositions Involving the Symbols $\forall, \exists$

### a) Universal Quantifier ($\forall$) and Existential Quantifier ($\exists$)

**Universal quantifier ($\forall$):** The symbol $\forall$ is read as "for all" or "for every". Example: $P: "\forall x \in \mathbb{R}, x^2 \ge 0"$.

**Existential quantifier ($\exists$):** The symbol $\exists$ is read as "there exists" (or "there is at least one"). Example: $Q: "\exists x \in \mathbb{Q}, x^2 = 2"$.

### b) Negation of Propositions Containing the Symbols $\forall, \exists$

**Negation of a proposition containing $\forall$:** The negation of the proposition "$\forall x \in X, P(x)$" is the proposition "$\exists x \in X, \overline{P(x)}$".

**Negation of a proposition containing $\exists$:** The negation of the proposition "$\exists x \in X, P(x)$" is the proposition "$\forall x \in X, \overline{P(x)}$".

**Example of negation:** The negation of $P: "\forall n \in \mathbb{N}, n \cdot 1 = n"$ is $\overline{P}: "\exists n \in \mathbb{N}, n \cdot 1 \ne n"$.

## History of Logic

**Aristotle (384 – 322 BC):** Propositional logic was first systematically developed by the Greek philosopher Aristotle over 2,300 years ago.

**George Boole (1815 – 1864):** Discussed and algebraicized by the English mathematician George Boole in 1854 in his book "The Laws of Thought", laying the foundation for Boolean algebra and modern computer science.
