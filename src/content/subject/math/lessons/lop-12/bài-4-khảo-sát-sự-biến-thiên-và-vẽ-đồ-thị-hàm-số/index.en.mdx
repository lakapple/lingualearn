## General Procedure for Curve Sketching

### a) Steps in Analyzing Variations and Graphing Functions

The general procedure for investigating and sketching the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ consists of three main stages:
1\. Determine the domain of the function.
2\. Investigate the variations of the function:
   • Compute the derivative $y'$. Find the critical points where $y' = 0$ or where the derivative is undefined.
   • Determine the sign of $y'$ to identify the intervals of increase and decrease (monotonicity).
   • Find the local extreme points and local extreme values of the function.
   • Evaluate limits at infinity and one-sided infinite limits to determine the asymptotes of the graph (vertical, horizontal, and slant/oblique asymptotes, if any).
   • Construct the table of variations summarizing the behavior of the function.
3\. Plot the graph of the function:
   • Identify key geometric points: local extreme points, intercepts with the coordinate axes (if easily computable), inflection points, and centers of symmetry.
   • Draw the asymptote lines (if any).
   • Sketch the curves guided by the table of variations and key points, paying attention to symmetries.

## Curve Sketching of Cubic Polynomial Functions

### a) General Form and Symmetry Properties

A cubic polynomial function has the general form: $y = ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d$ ($a \ne 0$).
• Domain: $D = \mathbb{R}$.
• Derivative: $y' = 3ax^2 + 2bx + c$ (a quadratic polynomial).
• The graph of a cubic polynomial always possesses a center of symmetry at its inflection point $I(x_0; y_0)$, where the abscissa $x_0$ satisfies $y'' = 0$, that is:
$$x_0 = -\frac{b}{3a}$$
• The graph of a cubic polynomial has no asymptotes.

### b) Illustrative Examples of Cubic Functions

**Example 1:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = -x^3 + 3x^2 - 4$.

Solution to Example 1:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Derivative: $y' = -3x^2 + 6x = -3x(x - 2)$. We have $y' = 0 \iff x = 0$ or $x = 2$.
  - Sign of $y'$: On $(0; 2)$, $y' > 0$, so the function is strictly increasing. On $(-\infty; 0)$ and $(2; +\infty)$, $y' < 0$, so the function is strictly decreasing.
  - Local extrema: The function attains a local minimum at $x = 0$ with $y\_{\min} = -4$; it attains a local maximum at $x = 2$ with $y\_{\max} = 0$.
  - Limits at infinity: $\lim\_{x \to -\infty} y = +\infty$; $\lim\_{x \to +\infty} y = -\infty$.
  - Table of variations:
    \* $x$ ranges from $-\infty$ through $0, 2$ to $+\infty$.
    \* $y'$ takes signs: negative, $0$, positive, $0$, negative.
    \* $y$ decreases from $+\infty$ to $-4$, increases to $0$, and decreases to $-\infty$.
• 3. Graph:
  - $y$-intercept: $(0; -4)$.
  - $x$-intercepts: $y = 0 \iff -(x - 2)^2(x + 1) = 0 \iff x = -1$ or $x = 2$, yielding $(-1; 0)$ and $(2; 0)$.
  - Center of symmetry: Second derivative $y'' = -6x + 6 = 0 \iff x = 1 \implies y(1) = -2$. Thus, the center of symmetry is $I(1; -2)$.

**Example 2:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = x^3 - 2x^2 + 2x - 1$.

Solution to Example 2:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Derivative: $y' = 3x^2 - 4x + 2$. The discriminant is $\Delta' = 4 - 6 = -2 < 0$ and $a = 3 > 0$, so $y' > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  - The function is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$ and has no local extrema.
  - Limits at infinity: $\lim\_{x \to -\infty} y = -\infty$; $\lim\_{x \to +\infty} y = +\infty$.
  - Table of variations: $y'$ is strictly positive; $y$ increases monotonically from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$.
• 3. Graph:
  - $y$-intercept: $(0; -1)$.
  - $x$-intercept: $y = 0 \iff (x - 1)(x^2 - x + 1) = 0 \iff x = 1 \implies (1; 0)$.
  - Center of symmetry: $y'' = 6x - 4 = 0 \iff x = \frac{2}{3} \implies y\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = -\frac{7}{27}$. The center of symmetry is $I\left(\frac{2}{3}; -\frac{7}{27}\right)$.

## Curve Sketching of Rational Functions

### a) Linear Fractional Rational Functions y = (ax + b)/(cx + d)

Consider the rational function $y = \frac{ax+b}{cx+d}$ ($c \ne 0, ad - bc \ne 0$):
• Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{-\frac{d}{c}\right\}$.
• Derivative: $y' = \frac{ad - bc}{(cx + d)^2}$. Since $y'$ maintains the constant sign of $ad - bc$ on each disconnected interval of its domain, the function is strictly monotonic on each interval and has no local extrema.
• Asymptotes:
  - Vertical asymptote: The vertical line $x = -\frac{d}{c}$.
  - Horizontal asymptote: The horizontal line $y = \frac{a}{c}$.
• Symmetries: The graph is a rectangular hyperbola having the intersection of its asymptotes $I\left(-\frac{d}{c}; \frac{a}{c}\right)$ as its center of symmetry, and the two angle bisectors of the asymptotes as its axes of symmetry.

**Example 3:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = \frac{x+1}{x-2}$.

Solution to Example 3:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \\{2\\}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Derivative: $y' = \frac{(x - 2) - (x + 1)}{(x - 2)^2} = -\frac{3}{(x - 2)^2} < 0, \forall x \ne 2$.
  - The function is strictly decreasing on each interval $(-\infty; 2)$ and $(2; +\infty)$. It has no local extrema.
  - Limits and asymptotes:
    $$\lim\_{x \to 2^-} y = -\infty; \quad \lim\_{x \to 2^+} y = +\infty \implies \text{Vertical asymptote: } x = 2.$$
    $$\lim\_{x \to \pm\infty} y = 1 \implies \text{Horizontal asymptote: } y = 1.$$
• 3. Graph:
  - Intercepts: $y$-intercept is $\left(0; -\frac{1}{2}\right)$; $x$-intercept is $(-1; 0)$.
  - Center of symmetry is the asymptote intersection $I(2; 1)$; the axes of symmetry are the bisector lines passing through $I$.

### b) Quadratic-Over-Linear Rational Functions y = (ax^2 + bx + c)/(px + q)

Consider the rational function $y = \frac{ax^2+bx+c}{px+q}$ ($a \ne 0, p \ne 0$, numerator not divisible by denominator):
• Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{-\frac{q}{p}\right\}$.
• Algebraic decomposition: $y = Ax + B + \frac{R}{px + q}$ where $A \ne 0, R \ne 0$.
• Asymptotes:
  - Vertical asymptote: $x = -\frac{q}{p}$.
  - Slant (oblique) asymptote: $y = Ax + B$.
• Symmetries: The graph has the intersection $I$ of the vertical and slant asymptotes as its center of symmetry, and the two angle bisectors of the asymptotes as its axes of symmetry.

**Example 4:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = \frac{x^2 - x - 1}{x - 2}$.

Solution to Example 4:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \\{2\\}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Decomposition: $y = x + 1 + \frac{1}{x - 2}$.
  - Derivative: $y' = 1 - \frac{1}{(x - 2)^2} = \frac{x^2 - 4x + 3}{(x - 2)^2}$.
  - Critical points: $y' = 0 \iff x = 1$ or $x = 3$.
  - Monotonicity: Increasing on $(-\infty; 1)$ and $(3; +\infty)$; decreasing on $(1; 2)$ and $(2; 3)$.
  - Extrema: Local maximum at $x = 1$ with $y\_{\max} = 1$; local minimum at $x = 3$ with $y\_{\min} = 5$.
  - Asymptotes: $\lim\_{x \to 2^-} y = -\infty$, $\lim\_{x \to 2^+} y = +\infty \implies$ Vertical asymptote: $x = 2$.
    Since $\lim\_{x \to \pm\infty} [y - (x + 1)] = \lim\_{x \to \pm\infty} \frac{1}{x - 2} = 0 \implies$ Slant asymptote: $y = x + 1$.
• 3. Graph:
  - Intercepts: $y$-intercept is $\left(0; \frac{1}{2}\right)$; $x$-intercepts are $x = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$.
  - Center of symmetry is the asymptote intersection: $I(2; 3)$.

## Applications and Exercises

### a) Real-World Applications

**Application 1 (Average Production Cost):** The total cost to produce $x$ units of a product is $C(x) = 2x + 45$ (million VND). The average cost per unit is given by $y = f(x) = \frac{C(x)}{x} = 2 + \frac{45}{x}$ ($x \ge 1$). Explain why the average cost decreases as $x$ increases but always remains greater than 2 million VND/unit.

Solution to Application 1:
• Consider the function $f(x) = 2 + \frac{45}{x}$ for $x \ge 1$:
  - Derivative: $f'(x) = -\frac{45}{x^2} < 0, \forall x \ge 1$. Hence, the average cost function is strictly decreasing on $[1; +\infty)$, showing that economies of scale reduce unit costs as output expands.
  - Because $x > 0$, we have $\frac{45}{x} > 0 \implies f(x) = 2 + \frac{45}{x} > 2$ for all $x \ge 1$.
  - Asymptotic behavior: $\lim\_{x \to +\infty} f(x) = \lim\_{x \to +\infty} \left(2 + \frac{45}{x}\right) = 2$.
• Geometric interpretation: The line $y = 2$ is a horizontal asymptote of the graph. As the production volume $x$ approaches infinity, the average cost curve approaches the line $y = 2$ from above but never intersects or drops below it.

**Application 2 (Chemical Disinfectant Dilution):** A tank initially holds 200 liters of pure water. Starting at time $t = 0$, water is pumped into the tank at a rate of 40 liters per minute, and simultaneously 20 grams of dissolved disinfectant are added each minute.
a) Determine the concentration of disinfectant $C(t)$ (in grams/liter) in the tank after $t$ minutes ($t \ge 0$).
b) Analyze the variations of the concentration function $C(t)$ and explain why the concentration increases over time but never exceeds $0.5\text{ g/L}$.

Solution to Application 2:
• a) After $t$ minutes:
  - Total volume of water in the tank: $V(t) = 200 + 40t$ (liters).
  - Total mass of dissolved disinfectant: $m(t) = 20t$ (grams).
  - The concentration function is:
    $$C(t) = \frac{m(t)}{V(t)} = \frac{20t}{200 + 40t} = \frac{t}{2t + 10}\text{ (g/L)} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{5}{2t + 10}.$$
• b) Monotonicity and asymptotic limit for $t \ge 0$:
  - Derivative: $C'(t) = \frac{10}{(2t + 10)^2} > 0, \forall t \ge 0$. Thus, $C(t)$ is strictly increasing on $[0; +\infty)$, meaning the chemical concentration rises continuously over time.
  - Limit at infinity: $\lim\_{t \to +\infty} C(t) = \lim\_{t \to +\infty} \frac{t}{2t + 10} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5\text{ (g/L)}$.
  - Since $2t + 10 > 0$, we have $C(t) = 0.5 - \frac{5}{2t + 10} < 0.5$ for all $t \ge 0$.
  - Consequently, the horizontal line $y = 0.5$ is a horizontal asymptote. The concentration approaches $0.5\text{ g/L}$ asymptotically from below but never exceeds this threshold.

### b) End-of-Lesson Exercises

**Exercise 1.21:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = -x^3 + 3x + 1$.

Solution to Exercise 1.21:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Derivative: $y' = -3x^2 + 3 = -3(x^2 - 1) = 0 \iff x = \pm 1$.
  - Increasing on $(-1; 1)$; decreasing on $(-\infty; -1)$ and $(1; +\infty)$.
  - Extrema: Local minimum at $x = -1, y\_{\min} = -1$; local maximum at $x = 1, y\_{\max} = 3$.
  - Limits: $\lim\_{x \to -\infty} y = +\infty; \lim\_{x \to +\infty} y = -\infty$.
• 3. Graph:
  - $y$-intercept: $(0; 1)$.
  - Inflection point and center of symmetry: $I(0; 1)$ (since $y'' = -6x = 0 \iff x = 0$).

**Exercise 1.22:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = \frac{2x+1}{x+1}$.

Solution to Exercise 1.22:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \\{-1\\}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Derivative: $y' = \frac{2(x + 1) - (2x + 1)}{(x + 1)^2} = \frac{1}{(x + 1)^2} > 0, \forall x \ne -1$.
  - The function is strictly increasing on $(-\infty; -1)$ and $(-1; +\infty)$, with no local extrema.
  - Vertical asymptote: $x = -1$ (since $\lim\_{x \to -1^-} y = +\infty, \lim\_{x \to -1^+} y = -\infty$).
  - Horizontal asymptote: $y = 2$ (since $\lim\_{x \to \pm\infty} y = 2$).
• 3. Graph:
  - Intercepts: $y$-intercept $(0; 1)$; $x$-intercept $\left(-\frac{1}{2}; 0\right)$.
  - Center of symmetry: The intersection of the asymptotes $I(-1; 2)$.

**Exercise 1.23:** Investigate the variations and sketch the graph of the function $y = \frac{2x^2 - x + 4}{x - 1}$.

Solution to Exercise 1.23:
• 1. Domain: $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \\{1\\}$.
• 2. Variations:
  - Decomposition: $y = 2x + 1 + \frac{5}{x - 1}$.
  - Derivative: $y' = 2 - \frac{5}{(x - 1)^2} = \frac{2(x - 1)^2 - 5}{(x - 1)^2}$.
  - Setting $y' = 0 \iff (x - 1)^2 = \frac{5}{2} \iff x = 1 \pm \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}$.
  - Increasing on $\left(-\infty; 1 - \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\right)$ and $\left(1 + \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}; +\infty\right)$;
    Decreasing on $\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}; 1\right)$ and $\left(1; 1 + \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\right)$.
  - Asymptotes: Vertical asymptote $x = 1$; slant asymptote $y = 2x + 1$.
• 3. Graph: The center of symmetry is the asymptote intersection $I(1; 3)$.

**Exercise 1.25 (Equivalent Resistance in a Parallel Circuit):** A fixed resistor of $8\\,\Omega$ is connected in parallel with a variable resistor of $x\\,\Omega$ ($x > 0$). The equivalent resistance of the circuit is given by $R(x) = \frac{8x}{x + 8}$. Analyze the variations of $R(x)$ and explain why the equivalent resistance never exceeds $8\\,\Omega$.

Solution to Exercise 1.25:
• Consider the function $R(x) = \frac{8x}{x + 8} = 8 - \frac{64}{x + 8}$ for $x > 0$:
  - Derivative: $R'(x) = \frac{64}{(x + 8)^2} > 0, \forall x > 0$.
  - Therefore, $R(x)$ is strictly increasing on $(0; +\infty)$, showing that increasing the resistance of the variable resistor increases the equivalent resistance of the parallel circuit.
  - Limit at infinity: $\lim\_{x \to +\infty} R(x) = \lim\_{x \to +\infty} \frac{8x}{x + 8} = 8\\,(\Omega)$.
  - For all $x > 0$, we have $x + 8 > 8 \implies \frac{64}{x + 8} > 0 \implies R(x) = 8 - \frac{64}{x + 8} < 8\\,(\Omega)$.
• Conclusion: As $x$ increases, the equivalent resistance increases asymptotically toward the horizontal asymptote line $y = 8$, but it can never exceed $8\\,\Omega$.
