### a) Planar Region Bounded by a Function Graph, the X-Axis, and Two Lines x = a, x = b

Theorem: The area $S$ of the region bounded by the graph of a continuous function $f(x)$ on the interval $[a; b]$, the x-axis ($y = 0$), and the two vertical lines $x = a$ and $x = b$ ($a < b$) is given by:
$$S = \int\_{a}^{b} |f(x)| dx$$
Method for evaluating integrals with absolute value:
• Find all roots $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k \in (a; b)$ of the equation $f(x) = 0$.
• Partition the interval $[a; b]$ into subintervals on which $f(x)$ does not change sign, so that:
  $$S = \int_a^{x_1} |f(x)| dx + \int\_{x_1}^{x_2} |f(x)| dx + \dots + \int\_{x_k}^b |f(x)| dx = \left| \int_a^{x_1} f(x) dx \right| + \left| \int\_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x) dx \right| + \dots + \left| \int\_{x_k}^b f(x) dx \right|$$

**Example 1:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the graph of the function $y = x^3$, the x-axis, and the two lines $x = 0, x = 2$.

Solution to Example 1:
• Since $y = x^3 \ge 0$ for all $x \in [0; 2]$, the required area is:
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{2} |x^3| dx = \int\_{0}^{2} x^3 dx = \frac{x^4}{4}\Bigg|\_{0}^{2} = \frac{2^4}{4} - 0 = 4.$$

**Example 2:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the function graph $y = \sin x$, the x-axis, and the two lines $x = 0, x = 2\pi$.

Solution to Example 2:
• Noting that $\sin x \ge 0$ on $[0; \pi]$ and $\sin x \le 0$ on $[\pi; 2\pi]$, the required area is:
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{2\pi} |\sin x| dx = \int\_{0}^{\pi} |\sin x| dx + \int\_{\pi}^{2\pi} |\sin x| dx = \int\_{0}^{\pi} \sin x dx + \int\_{\pi}^{2\pi} (-\sin x) dx$$
  $$= -\cos x\Big|\_{0}^{\pi} + \cos x\Big|\_{\pi}^{2\pi} = (-\cos \pi + \cos 0) + (\cos 2\pi - \cos \pi) = (1 + 1) + (1 - (-1)) = 4.$$

**Practice 1:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the parabola $y = x^2 - 4$, the x-axis, and the two lines $x = 0, x = 3$.

Solution to Practice 1:
• Solving $x^2 - 4 = 0$ on $[0; 3]$ yields $x = 2$.
• We observe: $x^2 - 4 \le 0$ on $[0; 2]$ and $x^2 - 4 \ge 0$ on $[2; 3]$.
• The area is:
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{3} |x^2 - 4| dx = \int\_{0}^{2} (4 - x^2) dx + \int\_{2}^{3} (x^2 - 4) dx$$
  $$= \left(4x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{2} + \left(\frac{x^3}{3} - 4x\right)\Bigg|\_{2}^{3} = \left(8 - \frac{8}{3}\right) + \left[(9 - 12) - \left(\frac{8}{3} - 8\right)\right] = \frac{16}{3} + \frac{7}{3} = \frac{23}{3}.$$

### b) Region Bounded by Two Function Graphs and Two Lines x = a, x = b

Theorem: The area $S$ of the region bounded by the graphs of two continuous functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ on $[a; b]$ and the two lines $x = a, x = b$ ($a < b$) is given by:
$$S = \int\_{a}^{b} |f(x) - g(x)| dx$$
Notes:
• If the difference $f(x) - g(x)$ maintains a constant sign on $[a; b]$, then:
  $$\int\_{a}^{b} |f(x) - g(x)| dx = \left| \int\_{a}^{b} [f(x) - g(x)] dx \right|$$
• When the bounding curves $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ do not provide preset limits $x = a, x = b$, we find the intersection abscissas by solving $f(x) = g(x)$, taking the smallest root as $a$ and the largest root as $b$.

**Example 3:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the two parabolas $y = 4 - x^2$, $y = x^2$, and the two lines $x = -1, x = 1$.

Solution to Example 3:
• For all $x \in [-1; 1]$, we have $(4 - x^2) - x^2 = 4 - 2x^2 \ge 2 > 0$.
• The area is:
  $$S = \int\_{-1}^{1} |(4 - x^2) - x^2| dx = \int\_{-1}^{1} (4 - 2x^2) dx = \left(4x - \frac{2}{3}x^3\right)\Bigg|\_{-1}^{1} = \left(4 - \frac{2}{3}\right) - \left(-4 + \frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{20}{3}.$$

**Example 4:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the function graphs $y = \sin x$, $y = \cos x$, and the two lines $x = 0, x = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

Solution to Example 4:
• On the interval $\left[0; \frac{\pi}{4}\right]$, we have $\cos x \ge \sin x \implies \cos x - \sin x \ge 0$.
• The area is:
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{\pi/4} |\sin x - \cos x| dx = \int\_{0}^{\pi/4} (\cos x - \sin x) dx = (\sin x + \cos x)\Bigg|\_{0}^{\pi/4} = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) - (0 + 1) = \sqrt{2} - 1.$$

**Practice 2:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the graphs of the functions $y = \sqrt{x}$, $y = x - 2$, and the two lines $x = 1, x = 4$.

Solution to Practice 2:
• Consider the difference $\sqrt{x} - (x - 2)$ on $[1; 4]$.
  Intersection equation: $\sqrt{x} = x - 2 \iff x = (x - 2)^2$ (for $x \ge 2$) $\iff x^2 - 5x + 4 = 0 \iff x = 4$ (the root $x = 1$ is extraneous since $x \ge 2$).
• For all $x \in [1; 4]$, we find $\sqrt{x} \ge x - 2$.
• The area is:
  $$S = \int\_{1}^{4} [\sqrt{x} - (x - 2)] dx = \left(\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^2}{2} + 2x\right)\Bigg|\_{1}^{4} = \left(\frac{16}{3} - 8 + 8\right) - \left(\frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{2} + 2\right) = \frac{16}{3} - \frac{13}{6} = \frac{19}{6}.$$

## Application of Definite Integrals to Compute Solid Volumes

### a) Volume of an Arbitrary Solid via Cross-Sectional Areas S(x)

Theorem: Let a solid in $Oxyz$ space be bounded by two planes perpendicular to the $Ox$-axis at $x = a$ and $x = b$ ($a < b$). Cutting the solid by a plane perpendicular to the $Ox$-axis at abscissa $x \in [a; b]$ yields a cross-section of area $S(x)$. Assuming $S(x)$ is continuous on $[a; b]$, the volume $V$ of the solid is given by:
$$V = \int\_{a}^{b} S(x) dx$$

**Example 5:** Compute the volume of a prism having a base area $S$ and height $h$.

Solution to Example 5:
• Choose the $Ox$-axis parallel to the altitude of the prism such that its two bases lie on planes perpendicular to $Ox$ at $x = 0$ and $x = h$.
• Cutting the prism by a plane perpendicular to $Ox$ at abscissa $x \in [0; h]$ yields a cross-section identical to the base, having a constant area $S(x) = S$.
• The volume of the prism is:
  $$V = \int\_{0}^{h} S(x) dx = \int\_{0}^{h} S dx = Sx\Big|\_{0}^{h} = Sh.$$

**Example 6:** Compute the volume of a regular pyramid with a square base of side length $L$ and height $h$.

Solution to Example 6:
• Choose the $Ox$-axis passing through the apex $O$ of the pyramid and perpendicular to the base plane at its center. Thus, the base lies at $x = h$.
• A plane perpendicular to $Ox$ at $x \in [0; h]$ intersects the pyramid in a square cross-section similar to the base, with side length $a$.
• By Thales' theorem: $\frac{a}{L} = \frac{x}{h} \implies a = \frac{L}{h}x$.
• The area of this cross-section at position $x$ is: $S(x) = a^2 = \frac{L^2}{h^2}x^2$.
• The volume of the pyramid is:
  $$V = \int\_{0}^{h} S(x) dx = \int\_{0}^{h} \frac{L^2}{h^2}x^2 dx = \frac{L^2}{h^2} \cdot \frac{x^3}{3}\Bigg|\_{0}^{h} = \frac{L^2}{h^2} \cdot \frac{h^3}{3} = \frac{1}{3}L^2 h.$$

### b) Volume of a Solid of Revolution Around the X-Axis

Definition and Formula:
Let $f(x)$ be a continuous and non-negative function on $[a; b]$. When the planar region bounded by the graph $y = f(x)$, the x-axis ($y = 0$), and the lines $x = a, x = b$ is revolved around the $Ox$-axis, it generates a solid of revolution.
• Cutting this solid by a plane perpendicular to $Ox$ at $x \in [a; b]$ yields a circular cross-section of radius $R = f(x)$, with area $S(x) = \pi f^2(x)$.
• The volume of the solid of revolution is given by:
$$V = \pi \int\_{a}^{b} f^2(x) dx$$

**Example 7:** Compute the volume of the solid of revolution generated by revolving around the $Ox$-axis the region bounded by $y = \sqrt{x}$, the x-axis, and the lines $x = 0, x = 1$.

Solution to Example 7:
• Applying the formula for the volume of a solid of revolution:
  $$V = \pi \int\_{0}^{1} (\sqrt{x})^2 dx = \pi \int\_{0}^{1} x dx = \frac{\pi x^2}{2}\Bigg|\_{0}^{1} = \frac{\pi}{2}.$$

**Example 8:** Prove the formula for the volume of a sphere of radius $R$.

Solution to Example 8:
• A sphere of radius $R$ is the solid of revolution generated by revolving the semicircle bounded by $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ ($-R \le x \le R$), the x-axis, and the lines $x = -R, x = R$ around the $Ox$-axis.
• The volume of the sphere is:
  $$V = \pi \int\_{-R}^{R} (\sqrt{R^2 - x^2})^2 dx = \pi \int\_{-R}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) dx = \pi \left(R^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)\Bigg|\_{-R}^{R}$$
  $$= \pi \left(R^3 - \frac{R^3}{3}\right) - \pi \left(-R^3 + \frac{R^3}{3}\right) = \pi \left(\frac{2R^3}{3} + \frac{2R^3}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3.$$

## Applications and Exercises

### a) Real-World and Economic Applications

**Application 1 (Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus):** Suppose the supply and demand functions for a product are modeled by:
• Demand: $p = D(x) = -0.36x + 9$;
• Supply: $p = S(x) = 0.14x + 2$;
where $x$ is the number of units and $p$ is the price. Find the consumer surplus ($CS$) and producer surplus ($PS$) at the market equilibrium point.

Solution to Application 1:
• The equilibrium point $(x_0; p_0)$ is the intersection of the demand and supply curves:
  $$-0.36x + 9 = 0.14x + 2 \iff 0.5x = 7 \iff x_0 = 14.$$
  Substituting into the supply function gives the equilibrium price: $p_0 = 0.14(14) + 2 = 1.96 + 2 = 3.96$.
• Consumer surplus ($CS$) is the area bounded by the demand curve $p = D(x)$, the horizontal line $p = p_0$, and the y-axis $x = 0$:
  $$CS = \int\_{0}^{x_0} [D(x) - p_0] dx = \int\_{0}^{14} [(-0.36x + 9) - 3.96] dx = \int\_{0}^{14} (5.04 - 0.36x) dx$$
  $$= \left(5.04x - 0.18x^2\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{14} = 5.04(14) - 0.18(14^2) = 70.56 - 35.28 = 35.28.$$
• Producer surplus ($PS$) is the area bounded by the horizontal line $p = p_0$, the supply curve $p = S(x)$, and the y-axis $x = 0$:
  $$PS = \int\_{0}^{x_0} [p_0 - S(x)] dx = \int\_{0}^{14} [3.96 - (0.14x + 2)] dx = \int\_{0}^{14} (1.96 - 0.14x) dx$$
  $$= \left(1.96x - 0.07x^2\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{14} = 1.96(14) - 0.07(14^2) = 27.44 - 13.72 = 13.72.$$

**Application 2 (Volume of a Regular Frustum of a Pyramid):** Compute the volume of a regular frustum of a pyramid having base areas $S_0$ and $S_1$ ($S_0 < S_1$) and height $h$. From this, derive the formula for the volume of a regular pyramid with base area $S$ and height $h$.

Solution to Application 2:
• Choose the $Ox$-axis passing through the center of both bases such that the smaller base lies at $x = a$ and the larger base lies at $x = b$ with $h = b - a$. The extended apex of the pyramid is the origin $O$.
• By similarity, the cross-sectional area at position $x$ is proportional to $x^2$: $S(x) = k x^2$.
  At $x = a: S_0 = k a^2 \implies a = \sqrt{\frac{S_0}{k}}$.
  At $x = b: S_1 = k b^2 \implies b = \sqrt{\frac{S_1}{k}}$.
  Height $h = b - a = \frac{\sqrt{S_1} - \sqrt{S_0}}{\sqrt{k}} \implies \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{S_1} - \sqrt{S_0}}$.
• The volume of the frustum is:
  $$V = \int_a^b S(x) dx = k \int_a^b x^2 dx = \frac{k}{3}(b^3 - a^3) = \frac{k}{3}(b - a)(b^2 + ab + a^2)$$
  $$= \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{h}{\sqrt{S_1} - \sqrt{S_0}} \cdot \left(\frac{S_1}{k} + \frac{\sqrt{S_0 S_1}}{k} + \frac{S_0}{k}\right) \cdot k = \frac{h}{3}(S_0 + \sqrt{S_0 S_1} + S_1).$$
• When $S_0 = 0$ (a standard regular pyramid with base $S_1 = S$), the formula reduces to $V = \frac{1}{3}Sh$.

### b) End-of-Lesson Exercises

**Exercise 4.14:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the parabola $y = 5x - x^2$ and the line $y = x$.

Solution to Exercise 4.14:
• Intersection equation: $5x - x^2 = x \iff x^2 - 4x = 0 \iff x = 0$ or $x = 4$.
• On the interval $[0; 4]$, we have $5x - x^2 \ge x$.
• The area of the region is:
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{4} [(5x - x^2) - x] dx = \int\_{0}^{4} (4x - x^2) dx = \left(2x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{4} = 2(16) - \frac{64}{3} = 32 - \frac{64}{3} = \frac{32}{3}.$$

**Exercise 4.15:** Compute the area of the region bounded by the following curves:
a) $y = e^x, y = x^2 - 1, x = -1, x = 1$;
b) $y = \sin x, y = x, x = \frac{\pi}{2}, x = \pi$;
c) $y = 9 - x^2, y = 2x^2, x = -\sqrt{3}, x = \sqrt{3}$;
d) $y = \sqrt{x}, y = x^2, x = 0, x = 1$.

Solution to Exercise 4.15:
• a) On $[-1; 1]$, $e^x > 0$ and $x^2 - 1 \le 0 \implies e^x \ge x^2 - 1$.
  $$S = \int\_{-1}^{1} [e^x - (x^2 - 1)] dx = \left(e^x - \frac{x^3}{3} + x\right)\Bigg|\_{-1}^{1} = \left(e - \frac{1}{3} + 1\right) - \left(e^{-1} + \frac{1}{3} - 1\right) = e - \frac{1}{e} + \frac{4}{3}.$$
• b) On $\left[\frac{\pi}{2}; \pi\right]$, we have $x > 1 \ge \sin x \implies x - \sin x > 0$.
  $$S = \int\_{\pi/2}^{\pi} (x - \sin x) dx = \left(\frac{x^2}{2} + \cos x\right)\Bigg|\_{\pi/2}^{\pi} = \left(\frac{\pi^2}{2} - 1\right) - \left(\frac{\pi^2}{8} + 0\right) = \frac{3\pi^2}{8} - 1.$$
• c) For all $x \in [-\sqrt{3}; \sqrt{3}]$, $9 - x^2 \ge 2x^2 \iff 3x^2 \le 9 \iff x^2 \le 3$.
  $$S = \int\_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} (9 - 3x^2) dx = \left(9x - x^3\right)\Bigg|\_{-\sqrt{3}}^{\sqrt{3}} = (9\sqrt{3} - 3\sqrt{3}) - (-9\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3}) = 12\sqrt{3}.$$
• d) On $[0; 1]$, $\sqrt{x} \ge x^2$.
  $$S = \int\_{0}^{1} (\sqrt{x} - x^2) dx = \left(\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{1} = \frac{2}{3} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{3}.$$

**Exercise 4.17:** Compute the volume of the solid of revolution generated by revolving around the $Ox$-axis the region bounded by the graph $y = 2x - x^2$ and the x-axis.

Solution to Exercise 4.17:
• Intersection with the x-axis: $2x - x^2 = 0 \iff x = 0$ or $x = 2$.
• The volume of the solid of revolution is:
  $$V = \pi \int\_{0}^{2} (2x - x^2)^2 dx = \pi \int\_{0}^{2} (4x^2 - 4x^3 + x^4) dx$$
  $$= \pi \left(\frac{4x^3}{3} - x^4 + \frac{x^5}{5}\right)\Bigg|\_{0}^{2} = \pi \left(\frac{32}{3} - 16 + \frac{32}{5}\right) = \pi \left(\frac{160 - 240 + 96}{15}\right) = \frac{16\pi}{15}.$$

**Exercise 4.18 (Volume of a Spherical Cap):** A spherical cap of radius $R$ and height $h$ ($0 < h \le R$) is generated by revolving the region bounded by the circular arc $y = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$, the x-axis, and the lines $x = R - h, x = R$ around the $Ox$-axis. Calculate the volume of this spherical cap.

Solution to Exercise 4.18:
• The volume of the solid of revolution is given by:
  $$V = \pi \int\_{R-h}^{R} (\sqrt{R^2 - x^2})^2 dx = \pi \int\_{R-h}^{R} (R^2 - x^2) dx = \pi \left(R^2 x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right)\Bigg|\_{R-h}^{R}$$
• Substituting the limits:
  - At $x = R$: $R^2(R) - \frac{R^3}{3} = \frac{2R^3}{3}$.
  - At $x = R - h$:
    $$R^2(R - h) - \frac{(R - h)^3}{3} = R^3 - R^2 h - \frac{R^3 - 3R^2 h + 3Rh^2 - h^3}{3} = \frac{2R^3}{3} - Rh^2 + \frac{h^3}{3}.$$
• Subtracting the two values:
  $$V = \pi \left[\frac{2R^3}{3} - \left(\frac{2R^3}{3} - Rh^2 + \frac{h^3}{3}\right)\right] = \pi \left(Rh^2 - \frac{h^3}{3}\right) = \pi h^2 \left(R - \frac{h}{3}\right).$$
• Thus, the volume of a spherical cap of radius $R$ and height $h$ is $V = \pi h^2\left(R - \frac{h}{3}\right)$.
